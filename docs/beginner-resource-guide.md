# Beginner Resource Guide

You took your first glass class. You made something. It may even be good. More likely it is charming in the way early glass is charming, which is to say slightly off center, heavier than expected, and somehow still completely worth keeping.

Now comes the real question: what next?

## The short version

Take another class. Learn to assist. Watch better people work. Keep a notebook. Do not buy equipment yet.

That last sentence is here because everyone ignores it.

## First 30 days

The goal of the first month is not independence. The goal is orientation.

- Write down what you made, who taught you, what words you did not understand, and what surprised you.
- Take the next appropriate class in the same process before hopping sideways into everything.
- Visit the studio when you are not making. Watching after you have tried it once is completely different from watching before.
- Learn the room. Where people stand. Where they do not stand. How hot things move. Who calls what, and when.
- Ask one useful question per session. "How do I get good?" is too big. "Where did I lose the heat?" is a question someone can answer.

## First 90 days

The goal of the first 90 days is repetition.

- Make a simple thing more than once.
- Assist someone better than you.
- Learn how work gets into and out of the annealer or kiln.
- Photograph your pieces, including the bad ones.
- Start learning cold work. The piece does not become finished just because the heat is done.
- Ask what the studio requires before rental time or independent work.

## First six months

The goal of the first six months is to pick a lane long enough to learn something real.

You can explore. You should explore. But glass has a way of punishing the person who wants to become competent at six processes at the same time while only practicing once a month.

Pick one for a while:

- Hot glass / glassblowing
- Flameworking
- Fusing and slumping
- Cold work
- Stained glass
- Casting

Then go deep enough to understand what the hard part actually is. It is almost never the part you thought was the hard part.

## Bay Area starting points

For San Francisco, [Public Glass](https://www.publicglass.org) is the obvious place to start. Their class paths include beginner classes, glassblowing, flameworking, fusing and slumping, coldworking, private lessons, youth classes, team building, and specialty workshops.

ArtGlass SF keeps a short resource list with Bay Area classes, suppliers, books, groups, and publications at https://artglasssf.com/glassblowing/.

## What not to buy yet

Do not buy a torch setup because you had one good bead class.

Do not buy a kiln because you fused one dish.

Do not buy hot-shop tools because they look beautiful and make you feel like the person you are trying to become.

That person is built at the bench, not on the checkout page.

Buy later, with advice, after you know what you are actually doing and where you are going to do it safely.

## What to practice

### Hot shop

- Gather the same amount three times in a row.
- Make three simple solid forms and compare them.
- Make cups without color so the form cannot hide behind the excitement.
- Practice punties as a team move, not a magic trick.
- Learn to use heat instead of muscle.

### Flameworking

- Pull stringers with consistent diameter.
- Make ten dots the same size.
- Make ten dots intentionally different sizes.
- Encapsulate without trapping soot, bubbles, or regret.
- Make something small, anneal it correctly, and check whether it survives tomorrow.

### Cold work

- Grind a bottom flat.
- Learn what each grit does.
- Do not move to the next grit until the previous scratch pattern is gone.
- Practice on pieces you do not love first.
- Learn when to stop. This is a transferable life skill, allegedly.

## How to be useful in the studio

- Say "behind" before moving behind someone.
- Do not surprise people holding hot glass.
- Ask before touching tools, glass, kilns, pipes, punties, or someone else's work.
- Keep your body predictable.
- Listen when someone says move.
- Clean up better than you found it.
- If you do not know what to do, stand clear and ask.

Being useful is not glamorous. Neither is a good punty. Both matter more than they get credit for.

## Questions worth asking

- What class should I take next?
- What skills do I need before rental time?
- What glass system does the studio use?
- Which colors are approved for that system?
- What are the kiln or annealer rules?
- How do I volunteer, assist, or watch more work?
- What did I do that looked fine but is going to cause trouble later?

That last one is a very good question. Ask it before the glass asks it for you.
