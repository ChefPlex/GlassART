# Kiln Schedule Reference

A kiln schedule is not a recipe you found on the internet and inflicted on expensive glass at midnight.

It is a controlled cooling and heating plan for a specific glass, in a specific kiln, at a specific thickness, for a specific result. Change one of those things and you may have changed the schedule.

## The short version

Use studio-approved schedules. Use manufacturer schedules. Log what actually happened. Do not promote a personal experiment to official policy because it worked once.

Once is not a system.

## Why annealing matters

Annealing is slow, controlled cooling. The point is to let stress relax before the glass reaches room temperature. If a hot glass object cools too quickly, it can hold strain and fail while cooling, during cold work, when bumped, or later when everyone has already decided it was fine.

Glass has a long memory and a terrible sense of timing.

## Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Segment | One programmed step in a kiln schedule. |
| Ramp rate | How fast the kiln changes temperature. Usually degrees per hour. |
| Target temperature | The temperature the segment is trying to reach. |
| Hold / soak | Time spent at a target temperature. |
| Annealing range | Range where stress can relax without losing the form. |
| Strain point | Lower region where stress relief becomes impractically slow. |
| Crash cool | A rapid cool to a safer range. Only use when the verified schedule calls for it. |
| Heatwork | The combined effect of temperature and time. Same top temperature, different outcome. Happens all the time. |

## Three kinds of schedules

Keep these separate.

| Type | Who owns it | How to treat it |
|---|---|---|
| Studio schedule | Studio / instructor / shop lead | Follow it. Do not edit casually. |
| Manufacturer schedule | Glass, kiln, or mold manufacturer | Use as a material-specific starting point. |
| Personal test schedule | You | Document it, label it as a test, and do not pretend it is universal. |

## Red flags

Stop and ask before firing when:

- You do not know the glass system.
- The piece is thicker than the verified schedule allows.
- The work includes unknown scrap, mixed systems, metal, stone, ceramic, mica, or found material.
- The kiln is unfamiliar.
- The mold is new, damp, cracked, or not properly prepared.
- The piece has uneven thickness, sealed air spaces, heavy bits, or sculptural attachments.
- The schedule came from a forum, a friend, or a PDF that does not know your kiln exists.

## Result vocabulary

| What Happened | What To Record |
|---|---|
| Cracked during firing | Segment, location, thickness, glass system, photo. |
| Cracked after pickup | Time delay, handling, coldworking, temperature change, crack path. |
| Slumped too far | Top temperature, hold, mold, thickness, shelf position. |
| Did not slump enough | Schedule, load, shelf position, kiln behavior, mold. |
| Devitrification | Cleaning, top temp, hold, atmosphere, glass system, shelf position. |
| Bubbles | Layer count, trapped air design, ramp, hold, bubble squeeze if used. |
| Sharp edges | Edge design, firing goal, top temperature, heatwork. |
